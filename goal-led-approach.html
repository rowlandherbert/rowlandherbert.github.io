Approach

A Goal-Led Approach to Technology Implementation

Technology promises to make work faster and more efficient. We see this in communications, industrial activity, and increasingly in the production of traditional white-collar deliverables. Yet the availability of a technological capability does not necessarily make it appropriate for a particular task.

Consider a food processor. It can grind, chop, and mince ingredients in bulk, but a cook may need a precise, consistent cut that is better achieved through skilled manual knifework. The appropriate intervention depends on the desired result, not simply on what the available technology can accomplish.

The same principle applies to organizational decision-making. A chatbot, for example, may be useful for retrieving or organizing information but poorly suited to replacing experienced, context-specific systems thinking.

Technology should serve the objective, rather than become the objective itself.

Integrating technology for its own sake can introduce unnecessary complexity and make proposed solutions more expensive than the problems they are intended to address. Beyond acquisition costs, organizations must account for the resources, relationships, and continuing obligations associated with development, implementation, integration, and maintenance.

This is especially relevant as organizations face increasing pressure to identify opportunities for artificial intelligence. Beginning with the technology risks producing solutions in search of problems, while overlooking interventions that may be more appropriate, less costly, or easier to sustain.

From Goals to Sustainable Implementation

My goal-led assessment reverses the technology-first approach.

It begins by defining the desired outcome and identifying the capabilities required to achieve it. It then evaluates how changes to organizational processes, institutional arrangements, or technology, including AI, could help close the gap between present conditions and the desired result.

Importantly, the assessment accounts for the risks, costs, resource requirements, and continuing obligations associated with different implementation pathways. Its purpose is not merely to identify a promising intervention, but to establish a realistic trajectory for implementing and sustaining it.

My approach draws on research and professional experience in service-led rail planning, transportation network analysis, transaction cost analysis, and risk management. These disciplines have reinforced a central principle in my work: effective implementation requires understanding both what an intervention can accomplish and what an institution must do to make it work.

The objective is to develop solutions that are appropriate to the task, feasible for the institution, and sustainable over time.
